## Intro to Palestine 1 ("Palestine Across the Ages") - read

URL: https://palestinetoday.quora.com/Palestine-Across-the-Ages-The-history-of-Palestine-is-vast-and-full-of-richness-Since-ancient-Egyptian-tablets-firs

Leads for the early eras (all need the underlying source, most likely Masalha, *Palestine: A Four Thousand Year History*, Zed Books 2018, ISBN 978-1-78699-272-7, page numbers to be found by the owner):
- Ancient Egyptian mention of "Peleset" more than 3,000 years ago; the name Palestine in wide use from the Late Bronze Age (from about 1300 BCE); candidates: the first written mention of Peleset (Egyptian inscriptions, c. 1175 BCE per the standard dating - to confirm in Masalha), Philistine coinage.
- Natufian culture, about 9,000 BCE, linked by the answer to later agricultural techniques (needs an archaeology source; the answer's dating is rounded).
- Assyrian, Nabataean, Persian and Roman layers; Roman, Byzantine and Islamic administrative recognition of Palestine.
- Jund Filastin: Umayyad and Abbasid coins with the mint name Filastin; al-Fustat Genizah manuscripts naming the Arab Muslim province of Filastin (candidate for `early-islamic`).

Notes for the Ottoman and Mandate entries already written:
- The answer says the Ottomans finished dividing Palestine into three sanjaks (Jerusalem, Nablus, Acre) in 1887, with about 600,000 people, about 10% Christian and about 25,000 Jews. That is a different date and total from the PalQuest figure already in `events.json` (462,465 in 1878) and from the 1872 mutasarrifate date. Different years and definitions; reconcile in the `ottoman-population-1878` and `jerusalem-mutasarrifate-1872` notes once the underlying source (McCarthy, below) is read.
- It names Daher al-Umar as a period of autonomy ended by Istanbul (already in the data), the Young Turk Revolution, Herzl and the First Zionist Congress (Basel, 1897 - not yet an event), Basel as a likely new Ottoman-era candidate.
- It frames Zionism as a settler-colonial movement and cites Herzl's 1902 letter to Cecil Rhodes and Jabotinsky's "Iron Wall" (1923) as evidence. Both are primary documents and would make good attributed sources for the event on the First Zionist Congress and for 1920s politics. The Iron Wall essay is a candidate for a separate event (1923) with the original text read.
